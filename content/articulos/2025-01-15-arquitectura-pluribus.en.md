At this point in the game, it's undeniable that the cinematography and settings in Vince Gilligan's series are especially well-crafted (Breaking Bad and Better Call Saul are good examples of this). His latest creation, the intriguing dystopia of Pluribus, offers a panorama inspired by contemporary society's technological advances that relies on science as a fundamental pillar, which makes possible in the plot the detection of an extraterrestrial signal from 600 light years away or work with the genome in advanced laboratories. Avant-garde or futuristic-tinged architecture usually accompanies plots of this nature to reinforce that sensation of thrilling novelty. This can be seen in films like Passengers, Ex Machina, or in many Black Mirror episodes.

<img width="1814" height="793" alt="fotogramapluribus1" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/628e4efc-88db-4590-9038-e6465edb104e" />

However, this doesn't happen in Pluribus, or at least not in the protagonist's domestic environment. Carol Sturka, a writer of romantic and fantasy novels, lives in a subdivision on the outskirts of Albuquerque where the houses share an aesthetic based on earthy tones that displays their wooden structures to the exterior, completely removed from the futuristic concept. Carol lives at the end of the cul-de-sac, the last house in the development, which somehow gives her the necessary uniqueness of the series' protagonist, but which in reality is just another mass-produced house in the suburban development model. Inside, wooden beams can be seen resting on corbels with volutes. The walls, between round arches, present light earth tones with softened and rounded edges more characteristic of traditional architecture than the sharp and rectilinear strokes of futuristic forms, and their materials reproduce renders and stuccos instead of pure whites and modern metallics.

Indeed, the protagonist's house is in the style that Americans agreed to call colonial. Specifically, it resembles the Castilian houses whose model the Spanish reproduced in America from the late 15th century. This house in New Mexico draws from past models by reproducing their identifying aesthetic elements. However, the organizational structure and distribution are foreign to those traditional models, so at its core this colonial style in Carol's house could be seen as an artifice, a historicist-tinged veneer on a contemporary subdivision house.

{% end %}

This can be interpreted as a dissonance sought by Gilligan, a touch of originality (yet another) in this dystopia, or perhaps he's trying to convey that the taste for these revivals in certain contexts is precisely a sign of our time and a forecast for the near future. The following image shows a ceiling of log beams and plaster vault, typical of traditional Spanish architecture. However, the large windows are a clear contribution from our time.

There's also an intention to show certain pavements applied in spaces that we wouldn't precisely consider the most noble in the house. Thus, in Carol's bathroom appears a very interesting hydraulic tile floor. Let's remember that this type of tile emerged in Spain in the mid-19th century and enriched salons and various rooms with its colorful and varied patterns.

{% end %}

In any case, it's certainly curious that the Calatrava-esque Bilbao airport was chosen in the series' second episode as the meeting place for characters of various nationalities. In this case, it does add a futuristic touch that moves away from the domestic and serves as a meeting scenario for a few individuals who have escaped the pluribus. The land from which the architecture reproduced in Albuquerque's houses originates becomes an emblem of modernity. Without a doubt, Gilligan's visual games and great style never cease to amaze me.
