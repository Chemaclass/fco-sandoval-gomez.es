San Miniato al Monte is a Romanesque basilica that has experienced the passage of time from a privileged promontory. This building was a witness to the construction of the enormous dome of Santa Maria del Fiore and the city's general transformation.

It itself changed over time, and although inside we can perceive that characteristic sensation of paleochristian basilicas where the Pantocrator mosaic stands out, other ornaments were added.

At this moment (February 2026) the facade and interior pavement cannot be appreciated because the building is immersed in restoration works, although fortunately I have some photos from the first time I visited this place, in July 2018. The combination of green and white marble is characteristic of Florence and is not relegated to quattrocento buildings. It is said that Alberti took references from this facade when designing Santa Maria Novella's (around 1460).

The legend about this temple's dedication is curious. It seems that San Miniato was Florence's first martyr, around the year 250, who was decapitated for declaring his faith in Christ. However, he took his head in his arms and ran to this hill, where he expressed his desire to be buried. Even more curious is that an identical legend exists about Saint Denis in Montmartre (Paris), although 22 years after San Miniato. In any case, whether we listen to the legend or observe the facade, we will perceive that history tends to repeat itself, and the iconic Romanesque facade of this basilica is full of classical elements, among which I want to highlight the window's pediment.

The point I'm making is no mystery to many, but believe me that for millennials (or millenarians, for those who have castellanized the term), who have grown up under the imperative of a creativity that seems to have forgotten tradition, it is not easy to look at History as just another link in the chain. And that is why these walls with visible putlog holes, which are so abundant in Italy, are so evocative to me. Putlog holes are holes in the walls where scaffolding was anchored during construction or repair.

And there lies the question: we not only build, but also repair, and we will not be the last to do so, because our intervention will have a lasting vocation, but it will not be eternal. There will be someone who must intervene again in the future.
